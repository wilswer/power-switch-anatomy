Background

The physics in brief

Six ideas explain almost everything in the drawings. Read them in order if the terms are new.

What a power switch has to do

A power converter chops electricity on and off thousands to millions of times per second. Each switch must block hundreds of volts with almost no leakage when off, carry tens to hundreds of amps with a small voltage drop when on, and move between the two in nanoseconds to microseconds. Every loss turns into heat.

Doping: n-type and p-type

Pure semiconductors barely conduct. A few impurity atoms change that. Atoms with one extra electron (phosphorus, nitrogen) donate free electrons: n-type. Atoms with one electron fewer (boron, aluminum) leave holes, missing electrons that act like positive carriers: p-type. A + means heavily doped and a − means lightly doped. The drawings color n-type blue and p-type red.

The pn junction and depletion

Where p-type meets n-type, electrons and holes near the boundary cancel out, leaving a depletion region with no free carriers. It behaves like an insulator. Make the n side positive (reverse bias) and the depletion region widens and holds off the voltage. The electric field inside it is strongest at the junction.

Bandgap and critical field

The bandgap is the energy needed to free an electron from its atom: 1.1 eV in silicon, about 3.3 eV in SiC and 3.4 eV in GaN. A wider bandgap allows a much higher field before freed electrons knock others loose and set off an avalanche. This critical field is about ten times higher in SiC and GaN, so their voltage-blocking layers can be about ten times thinner.

How a gate makes a channel

The gate is an electrode on a thin insulator, forming a capacitor with the semiconductor below. A positive gate voltage pulls electrons toward the surface of a p-type region until a thin layer of it behaves as n-type. That inversion channel connects source to drain. Remove the gate voltage and the channel disappears.

Unipolar versus bipolar

MOSFETs and HEMTs conduct with electrons only. They behave like resistors and switch off quickly, with no stored charge. An IGBT also injects holes, so both carriers flood its drift layer. That lowers its voltage drop at high current, but the extra charge has to be cleared at every turn-off, which slows it down.

Why there is no GaN IGBT

An IGBT works by flooding a thick drift layer with electrons and holes. In GaN they recombine within nanoseconds, because GaN has a direct bandgap (the same property that makes it good for LEDs). The flood never builds up. Good p-type GaN is also hard to make, since its magnesium dopants activate poorly, and affordable native GaN wafers for thick vertical devices are still emerging. So GaN power transistors are HEMTs. Silicon carbide IGBTs do exist, in research, for 10 kV and above.
