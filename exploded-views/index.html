Background

The physics in brief

Six ideas explain almost everything in the drawings. Read them in order if the terms are new.

What a power switch has to do

A power converter chops electricity on and off thousands to millions of times per second. Each switch must block hundreds of volts with almost no leakage when off, carry tens to hundreds of amps with a small voltage drop when on, and move between the two in nanoseconds to microseconds. Every loss turns into heat.

Doping: n-type and p-type

Pure semiconductors barely conduct. A few impurity atoms change that. Atoms with one extra electron (phosphorus, nitrogen) donate free electrons: n-type. Atoms with one electron fewer (boron, aluminum) leave holes, missing electrons that act like positive carriers: p-type. A + means heavily doped and a − means lightly doped. The drawings color n-type blue and p-type red.

The pn junction and depletion

Where p-type meets n-type, electrons and holes near the boundary cancel out, leaving a depletion region with no free carriers. It behaves like an insulator. Make the n side positive (reverse bias) and the depletion region widens and holds off the voltage. The electric field inside it is strongest at the junction.

Bandgap and critical field

The bandgap is the energy needed to free an electron from its atom: 1.1 eV in silicon, about 3.3 eV in SiC and 3.4 eV in GaN. A wider bandgap allows a much higher field before freed electrons knock others loose and set off an avalanche. This critical field is about ten times higher in SiC and GaN, so their voltage-blocking layers can be about ten times thinner.

How a gate makes a channel

The gate is an electrode on a thin insulator, forming a capacitor with the semiconductor below. A positive gate voltage pulls electrons toward the surface of a p-type region until a thin layer of it behaves as n-type. That inversion channel connects source to drain. Remove the gate voltage and the channel disappears.

Unipolar versus bipolar

MOSFETs and HEMTs conduct with electrons only. They behave like resistors and switch off quickly, with no stored charge. An IGBT also injects holes, so both carriers flood its drift layer. That lowers its voltage drop at high current, but the extra charge has to be cleared at every turn-off, which slows it down.

Background

Converters in brief

The grid delivers AC at a fixed voltage and frequency, but batteries, chips and LEDs need DC at other voltages, and motors run best on AC at a frequency of their own. A converter changes one form into another. There are four basic conversions, and real products chain them.

The four conversions

To DC To AC
From AC Rectifier. Diodes let current through one way only, so the AC comes out as pulsing DC that a capacitor smooths. An active rectifier uses switches instead, so it can draw a clean sine-wave current from the grid (power factor correction, PFC). In every charger and power supply, and at the front of every motor drive. AC-AC converter. To change the frequency, usually a rectifier and an inverter back to back, with a DC link between them. To change only the voltage at a fixed frequency, a plain transformer is enough. Motor drives, and the links that tie grids of different frequencies together.
From DC DC-DC converter. Steps a DC voltage up or down by chopping it into pulses and smoothing them with an inductor and a capacitor. With a transformer in the middle, it also insulates the output from the input. The second stage of a phone charger or an EV charger, the regulators next to a computer's processor, an EV's 12 V supply. Inverter. Makes AC from DC at any voltage and frequency, by switching each output between DC+ and DC− with pulse widths that rise and fall like a sine wave (pulse-width modulation). Motor drives, solar inverters, an electric car's traction inverter.

Why switch?

An ideal switch is either fully on, with current through it but no voltage across it, or fully off, with voltage across it but no current. Either way it turns no power into heat. A converter that only switches, and smooths the pulses with inductors and capacitors, can therefore change voltage while losing only a few percent. The alternative, a resistor or a transistor left half on, burns off the unwanted voltage: dropping 325 V to 20 V that way would turn 94 % of the power into heat. Faster, lower-loss switches like SiC and GaN allow higher switching frequencies, and so smaller inductors, capacitors and transformers.

Why it matters

A large and growing share of all electricity passes through converters on its way from where it is made to where it is used. Solar panels, batteries and many wind turbines feed the grid through inverters; every device with a plug draws its power through a rectifier and a DC-DC converter; electric cars, trains and most industrial motors run from inverters. A percent of loss saved in them adds up to whole power plants. The Application view at the top of the page shows three of them, taken apart.

Why there is no GaN IGBT

An IGBT works by flooding a thick drift layer with electrons and holes. In GaN they recombine within nanoseconds, because GaN has a direct bandgap (the same property that makes it good for LEDs). The flood never builds up. Good p-type GaN is also hard to make, since its magnesium dopants activate poorly, and affordable native GaN wafers for thick vertical devices are still emerging. So GaN power transistors are HEMTs. Silicon carbide IGBTs do exist, in research, for 10 kV and above.

Reading the label and the datasheet

The marking on a package is a part number. Its datasheet opens with the voltage rating (VDS or VCE), the on-resistance RDS(on) or saturation voltage VCE(sat), a current rating and Rth(j-c). The headline current usually assumes the case is held at 25 °C, which no real heatsink manages, so parts are used well below it. That is the Heat view in practice: what a part can carry is set by how fast its package sheds heat. The last pages show the package drawing and which leg is which.

Before you open anything

The safe way to hold one is to buy a single TO-247 SiC MOSFET or a GaN part from an electronics distributor; they cost a few dollars. Chargers and power supplies hold large capacitors that can stay charged to hundreds of volts long after they are unplugged and give a dangerous shock. Never open anything that is plugged in, treat the capacitors as charged, and leave EV, solar and mains-wired equipment to qualified people. Teardown photos and videos are the easy alternative.

Reliability

Failure modes

How these devices fail in service, what you can measure as they age, and how manufacturers and designers keep it from happening. Each card links to the part in the 3D views, and part cards there list the ways that part can fail.

How lifetime is estimated

  1. Mission profile: the load and ambient temperature over years of use, such as a drive cycle for a car or a year of sun for a solar inverter.
  2. Electro-thermal model: the losses, through the package's thermal resistances (as in the Heat view), give the junction temperature over time.
  3. Cycle counting: rainflow counting splits that temperature history into cycles, each with a swing ΔTj, a mean or minimum and a duration.
  4. Lifetime model: a model fitted to power-cycling tests, such as the CIPS08 model, gives the cycles to failure for each kind of cycle.
  5. Damage sum: Miner's rule adds up the fraction of life each cycle uses; a sum of 1 means end of life. It assumes damage simply adds up, whatever the order of the cycles, which is an approximation. Monte Carlo over the scatter of the parameters turns that into a failure probability, for example the time by which 10 % have failed (B10).

Watching devices age

Condition monitoring tracks health indicators in operation: the on-state voltage (lifted bond wires), the thermal resistance (cracked solder), the threshold voltage and gate leakage (SiC and GaN gates), and capacitor ESR. The difficulty is that all of them also change with temperature and load, so degradation has to be separated from operating point, by measuring under reference conditions or with a model of the healthy device. The junction temperature itself is read through temperature-sensitive electrical parameters, such as the voltage drop at a small sense current.

Qualification

Before a device is sold, samples go through standard stress tests that accelerate each mechanism: for example the JEDEC JESD22 test methods, AEC-Q101 for automotive discretes, ECPE AQG 324 for automotive power modules, IEC 60749-34 for power cycling, and JEDEC JEP173 and JEP180 for GaN. Passing them shows a device survives a given stress, not how long it will last in a particular use; that takes the mission-profile approach.
